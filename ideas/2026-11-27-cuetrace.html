CueTrace

Drop in a locked edit and the composer's stems; CueTrace fingerprints every music cue in the final mix and files a PRO-ready cue sheet, so indie composers stop losing the performance royalties they already earned.

Target Customer

Independent film, documentary, and unscripted-TV composers (and the small post-production houses and music supervisors who hire them) whose work airs on broadcast, cable, streaming, festivals, or monetized YouTube — typically productions with budgets from $50K to $3M that have no studio music-clearance department.

Pain Point

Performance-rights organizations (ASCAP, BMI, SESAC, PRS, SOCAN and others) pay composers for broadcast and streaming use of their music only when a cue sheet exists: a precise list of every cue with title, start/end timecode, duration, usage type (background, visual vocal, theme), writers, publishers, and splits. Big studios have staff or enterprise vendors for this. On indie projects nobody owns it — the editor is gone, the producer doesn't know it's required, and the composer is left reconstructing timings by scrubbing a screener with a stopwatch, often months after delivery. Cue sheets get filed late, wrong (cues re-cut or looped in the final mix no longer match the composer's original lengths), or never. The result is quiet, compounding loss: a single documentary that airs internationally for years can be worth thousands in royalties that simply never get claimed, and distributors increasingly reject deliverables packages that are missing a cue sheet.

MVP Workflow

  1. Composer creates a project and uploads (or links via Frame.io/Dropbox) the final locked picture or mixed audio, plus a folder of their delivered cue stems/bounces.
  2. Audio fingerprinting matches each stem against the final mix, detecting every occurrence — including cues that the editor trimmed, looped, layered under dialogue, or reused in a different scene — and records precise in/out timecodes and durations.
  3. An editor's EDL/AAF/XML (optional) is parsed to cross-check timings and catch licensed library or third-party tracks the composer didn't write, which are flagged separately for the producer.
  4. A classifier proposes the usage type for each occurrence (background vs. visual vocal vs. main/end title theme) using placement cues — opening/closing position, on-screen performance, dialogue presence — which the composer confirms with one click.
  5. Writer, publisher, PRO affiliation, IPI numbers, and splits are filled from the composer's saved profile and collaborator directory.
  6. CueTrace exports a cue sheet in the formats PROs and distributors accept (PDF, Excel templates, and structured files for upload to cue-sheet portals), plus a reminder schedule to verify that registrations appear on royalty statements after air dates.

Data & Integrations Needed

Audio fingerprinting (open-source Chromaprint / custom landmark hashing) EDL / AAF / FCPXML / Premiere XML parsers Frame.io, Dropbox, Google Drive file import PRO cue-sheet templates & portal export formats Composer profile: IPI/CAE numbers, publishers, splits Air-date tracking & royalty-statement reminder engine

Pricing Model

Per-project pricing for occasional users: $49 for a feature or episode, with series packs (10 episodes for $299). A $29/mo "Working Composer" plan includes unlimited cue sheets, saved collaborator splits, and the air-date tracker. A $149/mo studio tier for post houses and music supervisors adds multi-composer projects and deliverables-package exports for distributors.

First Version Scope

Audio-only fingerprint matching between uploaded stems and one final mix file, timecode output, a confirm-and-edit review table, and PDF/Excel cue sheet export in the ASCAP/BMI-accepted layout. Skip EDL parsing, library-track detection, and portal-specific structured uploads until v1.1. English-language PROs (US, UK, Canada) only. Go to market through composer communities (film-scoring forums, subreddits, Discord groups), film-scoring programs, the Society of Composers & Lyricists, and festival composer panels — where "I've never filed a cue sheet for that doc" is a common confession.

What Makes It Non-Obvious

The hard data problem — "where exactly does each of my cues appear in the finished picture, at what length, and how was it used?" — is a precise signal-matching task, not a language task, and the composer is the only party who already holds the reference audio needed to solve it. That inverts the usual workflow: instead of waiting on a busy editor for an EDL that may not reflect the final mix, the person who gets paid can produce the document themselves in minutes. Enterprise cue-sheet vendors sell top-down to studios and broadcasters; nobody sells bottom-up to the thousands of independent composers for whom one forgotten cue sheet is real money. And because royalties keep paying for years after air, the ROI pitch is unusually concrete: the product's price is often recovered by a single international broadcast.
