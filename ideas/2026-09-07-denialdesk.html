SaaS Idea Log — 2026-09-07

DenialDesk

A denial-code-aware appeal engine that turns a solo therapist's insurance denial letter into a payer-formatted, deadline-tracked appeal in minutes, instead of a write-off.

Target Customer

Solo and small-group behavioral health practices — licensed therapists, psychologists, and LCSWs who bill insurance directly and have no dedicated billing staff — typically 1–5 clinicians using SimplePractice, TherapyNotes, or a similar EHR without a real revenue-cycle module.

Pain Point

Mental health claims get denied at a disproportionately high rate — missing prior auth, "not medically necessary," wrong modifier, exceeded session limits — and each denial arrives as a cryptic EOB with CARC/RARC codes that most clinicians can't decode without training. Appealing correctly means matching the denial to the right payer's appeal format and deadline (often 30–90 days, varies by plan) and writing a clinically defensible medical-necessity justification. A single session denial is worth $80–$200, too small to justify hiring a biller or a $50K/year RCM platform built for hospitals — so the default behavior is to eat the loss, and a solo practice can be quietly losing thousands per year this way.

MVP Workflow

  1. Clinician forwards the denial EOB/letter (PDF or photo) to a dedicated inbound email address, or uploads it in the app.
  2. OCR plus a CARC/RARC lookup table extracts payer, denial reason code, patient/claim reference, and the appeal deadline implied by that payer's plan documents.
  3. The system classifies the denial into one of a handful of common patterns (missing auth, medical necessity, timely filing, coding error, session-limit exceeded) and pulls the matching payer-specific appeal template.
  4. Clinician answers 2–3 short structured prompts (diagnosis, treatment plan summary, why continued care is necessary) instead of writing prose from scratch; the system assembles a complete, correctly formatted appeal letter/PDF citing the specific denial code.
  5. Dashboard tracks every open appeal by deadline, auto-reminds the clinician before it lapses, and logs outcomes so the practice can see which payers deny most and why.

Data & Integrations Needed

OCR / document AI for EOBs Public X12 CARC/RARC code tables Payer-specific appeal-format & deadline library (top 8–10 payers) Inbound email parsing (Mailgun/SendGrid) PDF generation Later: EHR export/import (SimplePractice, TherapyNotes)

Pricing Model

$59/month per clinician for unlimited appeals, or $19 per one-off appeal for practices that want to try before subscribing. Positioned against the dollar value of a single recovered claim, not against enterprise RCM software, so the ROI case is obvious after the first appeal it helps win.

First Version Scope

Manual upload/forward of denial letters only (no live EHR or clearinghouse integration yet). Cover the 5 most common denial patterns and the 8–10 payers that dominate outpatient mental health billing (UnitedHealthcare/Optum, Aetna, Cigna/Evernorth, BCBS regional plans, Medicaid MCOs). Output is a downloadable, submission-ready PDF the clinician mails or uploads to the payer portal themselves — no automated fax/submission in v1. Go to market through therapist Facebook groups, private-practice subreddits, and referrals from practice-management consultants who already advise solo clinicians on billing.

What Makes It Non-Obvious

Healthtech billing tools overwhelmingly target hospitals and large medical groups with dedicated billing departments; solo behavioral health clinicians are ignored because each denial is small money, even though in aggregate this is one of the largest, most underserved provider populations in U.S. healthcare. The product also isn't a generic "AI writes your letter" chatbot — its value is the structured domain layer (the CARC/RARC taxonomy plus a maintained library of payer-specific appeal formats and deadlines) that a clinician typing into ChatGPT has no way to assemble correctly on their own, and getting a single field wrong is exactly what gets an appeal rejected on a technicality.
