CatchLedger

AI reconciles a small-boat captain's landing receipts against their NOAA individual fishing quota balance in real time, so a good trip doesn't turn into a federal overage violation and a frozen permit.

Target Customer

Independent commercial fishing captains and owner-operators (1–3 vessel businesses) working in catch-share/IFQ (individual fishing quota) managed fisheries — Alaska halibut and sablefish, Gulf of Mexico red snapper and grouper, Atlantic sea scallop and surfclam — who hold and lease quota shares but have no back-office staff or compliance department.

Pain Point

Catch-share programs assign each permit holder a hard pound allocation per species, area, and season; landings must be reported to a licensed dealer and logged against the running balance, and any overage triggers a NOAA Office of Law Enforcement violation, fines that commonly run into five figures, and an automatic deduction from next season's quota. Captains often hold quota across several species and statistical areas, buy or lease additional pounds mid-season from other quota-share holders, and track it all against paper landing tickets, dealer settlement sheets, and the government's own eLandings/IFQ portal — none of which reconcile with each other in real time or work well from a boat with no signal. The result is captains guessing at their remaining balance on the last trips of a season, which is exactly when overages happen and livelihoods (the permit itself) are on the line.

MVP Workflow

  1. Captain links their NOAA IFQ/eLandings account (or grants read access) so the system pulls current quota allocation and any lease/transfer activity per species and area.
  2. After each landing, the captain photographs the dealer settlement sheet or scale ticket — offline-capable capture that syncs once back in cell range.
  3. OCR extracts species, weight, area, and date, and the system updates a running "pounds remaining" balance per species/area against the linked quota.
  4. Before the balance would go negative on a projected trip (using recent average landings per trip as the estimate), the captain gets a push/SMS alert to hold back, land less, or lease more quota before going out again.
  5. At season close, the system produces a reconciliation report matching every logged landing to its dealer receipt and the NOAA balance, so any discrepancy is caught and disputed before it becomes an enforcement case.

Data & Integrations Needed

NOAA eLandings / IFQ portal data Dealer settlement sheet OCR Offline-first mobile capture Quota lease/transfer records SMS/push alerting Per-species/area balance engine Season-end reconciliation reports

Pricing Model

Per-vessel monthly subscription during the active season, ~$75–150/month per permit, with an add-on per additional species/area tracked beyond the first two; priced to undercut a single avoided overage fine many times over.

First Version Scope

Launch with a single, well-documented fishery — Alaska halibut and sablefish IFQ, which already has structured NOAA data — supporting manual landing-ticket photo upload plus NOAA balance polling and the running-balance dashboard with overage alerts. No automated quota lease/trading integration yet; captains log leases manually. Recruit 15–20 pilot vessels through fishermen's co-ops, permit brokers, and dockside associations in Homer, Kodiak, and Sitka.

What Makes It Non-Obvious

Fisheries compliance software exists, but it's built for processors, dealers, and regional management councils — nobody builds the pocket version for the solo captain who is technically excellent at running a boat and terrible at reconciling three spreadsheets against a federal portal with no cell signal. The underlying data (NOAA catch-share allocations, landing reports) is public and structured, which makes this a tractable OCR-plus-balance-reconciliation problem rather than a conversational one — a chatbot wrapper adds nothing here, but an offline-capable capture-and-alert system that understands catch-share rules directly prevents a fine. It also sits outside where software attention usually goes in fishing (gear, weather, market apps), in the unglamorous regulatory-accounting layer where the actual business risk lives.
