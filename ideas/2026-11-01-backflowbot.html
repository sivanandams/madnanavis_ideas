BackflowBot

Turns a plumber's field test readings into the exact compliance form each water utility demands, files it in the right portal, and chases every device's annual retest date so no customer's water gets shut off.

Target Customer

Certified backflow-prevention testers: solo plumbers and 1–10 person shops that test commercial and irrigation backflow assemblies (RPZ, double-check, PVB) under contract for restaurants, HOAs, property managers, and landscapers. Typically 300–3,000 devices under management, spread across 5–40 different water utilities.

Pain Point

Nearly every water utility requires an annual backflow test, and each one has its own form, its own submission method (paper, email, a clunky portal, or a third-party tracker), and its own late-notice rules. Testers lose hours every week re-typing the same gauge readings into different templates, and missed or rejected submissions mean the customer gets a shutoff warning and blames the tester. Existing tracking software is built for the utilities, not for the testers doing the field work, so testers still run on spreadsheets, paper tags, and memory. A tester who loses a handful of accounts a year to administrative failure loses real recurring revenue.

MVP Workflow

  1. Tester enters readings on a phone-first form in the field (device ID, make/model, serial, check valve pressures, relief valve opening point, pass/fail, repairs made). Offline-capable, with photo of the device tag.
  2. The system looks up the device's utility, selects that utility's form template, and auto-fills every field, including tester certification number, gauge calibration date, and customer account number.
  3. It outputs a ready-to-submit PDF or portal-formatted payload and either emails it to the utility's intake address or opens a pre-filled portal session for one-tap submission. Submission status is tracked (sent, accepted, rejected with reason).
  4. A tiny AI step reads rejection emails and notices, extracts the reason (e.g. "gauge calibration expired"), and creates a fix task.
  5. A retest engine schedules each device's next due date per utility rules and sends the customer an automatic booking link 60 / 30 / 7 days out, so the tester's calendar fills itself.
  6. Customer-facing status page shows "compliant through [date]" for each device, which property managers can forward to the utility or insurer.

Data & Integrations Needed

Utility form library (hand-built for the first ~25 utilities in one metro) Gauge calibration tracker Tester certification records Twilio SMS / email for reminders Stripe for customer booking deposits Google/Outlook calendar sync LLM form-field mapping and rejection-notice parsing QuickBooks / Jobber export

Pricing Model

$49/mo for solo testers (up to 500 devices) and $129/mo for shops (unlimited devices, 5 testers, calendar sync). Optional $1 per auto-submitted report beyond a monthly allowance. Testers recover the cost with a single saved hour a week or one retained account.

First Version Scope

One metro area and its 10–15 utilities. Mobile test-entry form, template auto-fill for those utilities' forms, email submission only (no portal automation yet), device and customer list with due-date reminders, and a CSV import of the tester's existing spreadsheet. Skip invoicing, repair quoting, and portal scraping for v1; add portal assistance once the form library proves useful.

What Makes It Non-Obvious

The paying customer is the tester, not the utility, which is the opposite of the existing compliance-tracking vendors. The moat is a boring, hand-curated library of utility-specific forms and rules that compounds with every new utility added, and it is too small and too local for big vendors to bother with. AI is used where it is genuinely useful (mapping messy forms, parsing rejection notices), not as a chatbot. The same data model extends naturally to fire-sprinkler inspectors, grease-trap haulers, and elevator inspectors, who face the same many-regulators, one-set-of-readings problem.
