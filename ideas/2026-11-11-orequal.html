OrEqual

AI reads a construction spec section, lines it up against your product's data sheets and test reports, and assembles a complete "or-equal" substitution request package before the pre-bid deadline closes.

Target Customer

Independent manufacturer's reps, specialty distributors, and inside-sales teams at small-to-mid building-product manufacturers (roofing membranes, door hardware, flooring, louvers, sealants, HVAC accessories, lighting) whose products are routinely not the basis-of-design and must win their way onto projects through substitution requests to architects and engineers.

Pain Point

Architects often write specs around one named product with "or approved equal" language, and a substitution is only accepted if a formal request (usually CSI Form 13.1A or the architect's own form) arrives before a cutoff — often 7–10 days before bid. Each package means reading a 15–40 page spec section, pulling out every Part 2 performance requirement (ASTM values, fire ratings, warranties, finishes, LEED/EPD needs), finding matching evidence across the product's data sheets and test reports, writing a point-by-point comparison, and noting any deviations. A rep spends 4–8 hours on one package, watches dozens of bids a month, and skips most of them. Each skipped spec is a whole project's material order, often $20K–$500K, quietly handed to the competitor whose name is in the spec.

MVP Workflow

  1. Rep uploads (or forwards by email) the project spec section PDF and enters the bid date and substitution deadline; the system starts a deadline countdown with reminders.
  2. A parser built for CSI MasterFormat/SectionFormat extracts Part 1 submittal rules, Part 2 named products and every measurable requirement into a structured checklist (property, test method, threshold, unit).
  3. The rep's product library (data sheets, ICC-ES/UL reports, ASTM test results, warranties, EPDs), indexed once at onboarding, is matched against each requirement, with the exact source page cited for every value.
  4. A comparison matrix shows each line as Meets / Exceeds / Deviates / No evidence, side by side with the basis-of-design product's published values, and lists gaps the rep must fix or explain.
  5. One click builds the submission: a filled-in CSI 13.1A (or the architect's form), the comparison matrix, highlighted supporting documents, and a cover letter, merged into one bookmarked PDF.
  6. Outcomes (accepted, rejected, reason) are logged per architect firm, so the next package for that firm starts with its known preferences and common reasons for rejection.

Data & Integrations Needed

Spec PDF parsing (CSI MasterFormat / SectionFormat) Manufacturer data sheets, ICC-ES, UL & ASTM reports Competitor basis-of-design data sheets (public) CSI Form 13.1A template Plan-room feeds (ConstructConnect, Dodge, BuildingConnected) — later EPD / HPD databases (EC3, HPDC) for sustainability specs PDF assembly & highlighting CRM sync (HubSpot, Salesforce)

Pricing Model

$199/month per rep seat with 15 packages included and $25 per extra package. A manufacturer tier at $1,500–$3,000/month gives a shared product library, rep-network access, and win/loss reports by region and architect. One accepted substitution on a mid-size project pays for a year.

First Version Scope

One product category at launch (single-ply roofing or Division 08 door hardware, where specs are dense with ASTM values), one fixed output (CSI 13.1A plus matrix plus merged PDF), and manual spec upload only; no plan-room scraping yet. Product libraries are onboarded by hand for the first 10 customers to tune the matcher. Sell through rep-agency associations (MANA), CSI chapter meetings, and LinkedIn outreach to regional sales managers at challenger brands.

What Makes It Non-Obvious

Construction-tech money flows to general contractors and owners. The sell side of the spec, the challenger manufacturer trying to get unspecified, is ignored even though it has the clearest return: every accepted package is a direct, traceable sale. Spec sections are also far more structured than they look (a fixed three-part format, numbered articles, standard test methods), so the core job is careful extraction and evidence matching with citations, not open-ended chat, and architects can check it line by line. Over time the accept/reject log per architect firm becomes data nobody else has: which firms really accept equals, on which properties, and how they like to see them presented.
