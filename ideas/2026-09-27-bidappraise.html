SaaS Idea Log — 2026-09-27

BidAppraise

A pre-bid appraisal tool for salvage-auction vehicle buyers: enter a VIN and the lot's damage photos, get a repair-cost estimate, comparable resale value, and a max-bid ceiling before the Copart/IAAI countdown hits zero.

Target Customer

Independent used-car dealers, licensed vehicle rebuilders, and small export buyers (many shipping rebuilt units to Latin America, West Africa, and Eastern Europe) who actively bid on salvage-title vehicles at Copart and IAAI auctions — typically buying 10–100+ vehicles a month, often bidding on several lots a day with only a photo set, a VIN, and a short damage narrative to go on.

Pain Point

Salvage auction lots close on a fixed clock with no test drive, no lift inspection, and photos that are frequently low-resolution, poorly angled, or missing the underside/frame damage that actually determines total cost to rebuild. Bidders either guess (and regularly overpay on a vehicle with hidden frame or airbag-module damage that turns a "$1,200 repair" into a $4,000 one) or pay $150–$300 per vehicle for a third-party pre-bid inspection service that can't turn results around inside the auction's short preview window. At real buying volumes, a handful of bad bids a month wipes out the margin on everything else, and there is no fast, cheap, always-on way to sanity-check a bid before clicking.

MVP Workflow

  1. Buyer enters the VIN and manually uploads the lot's photo set (or pastes the public Copart/IAAI lot number and photo URLs) along with the listed damage type, primary damage location, and odometer/keys/runs-drives status.
  2. VIN decode (NHTSA vPIC) pulls trim, engine, drivetrain, and factory options to anchor the valuation to the exact build, not just year/make/model.
  3. A vision model trained on labeled collision-damage photo sets scores damage severity by zone (front clip, frame rail, airbags/SRS, radiator support, unibody rail) and flags photos that don't show enough of the car to trust the read — pushing the buyer to request specific angles before bidding.
  4. A parts-and-labor cost engine maps the damage zones to an OEM/aftermarket parts list and regional labor-rate table to produce a repair-cost range with a confidence band, not a false-precision single number.
  5. A comparables engine pulls recent clean-title and rebuilt-title sale prices for the same VIN configuration (dealer auction comps plus regional classifieds) to estimate resale value once repaired.
  6. The system outputs a max-bid ceiling (resale value minus repair cost minus target margin minus transport/title fees) with a one-line rationale, refreshed instantly if the buyer edits any input — fast enough to use live during the auction's closing seconds.

Data & Integrations Needed

NHTSA vPIC VIN decode (free) Labeled collision-damage photo dataset + CV severity model Aftermarket/OEM parts pricing feed Regional labor-rate table Dealer auction & classifieds comp data Manual lot/photo intake (no auction-site scraping in v1)

Pricing Model

Pay-per-lot credits ($6–$9 per vehicle appraised) for occasional bidders, plus an unlimited monthly plan ($149–$399/mo tiered by volume) for dealers and rebuilders running dozens of lots a week. A per-vehicle price point matches how buyers already think about cost — each appraisal is cheap insurance against one bad bid.

First Version Scope

Launch with front-end collision damage only on the 8–10 highest-volume rebuild models (F-150, Silverado, Camry, Accord, Civic, Altima, Corolla, RAV4) in a single auction region, using manually uploaded photos and lot data to sidestep any auction-site scraping or ToS questions. Flood, fire, hail, and rollover categories — which need different damage signals (water lines, smoke residue, roof-crush geometry) — are explicitly out of scope until the core CV model is validated against real post-repair invoices from a small panel of partner rebuild shops.

What Makes It Non-Obvious

Body shops and independent appraisers already know how to price collision damage from photos — but their business model bills by the hour for a manual estimate, so none of them have built software to compress that judgment into a 60-second, $7 read delivered at the exact moment a bidder needs it: mid-auction, with the clock running. The product isn't competing with a shop estimate on precision; it's competing with "guess or skip the lot," which is the real alternative most bidders face today. And because salvage buyers evaluate dozens of lots a week, the per-vehicle pricing model turns every single bid into a repeat-purchase moment instead of a one-time sale, which is a much stronger usage-based revenue engine than the subscription-and-hope model most inspection tools default to.
